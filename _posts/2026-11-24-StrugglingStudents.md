# What I wish I could tell the families of students who seem unwilling to do the work we know they can do

I have been teaching since 2006: children in K-12 schools, undergraduates, and graduate students in three countries. One situation comes up again and again. A young adult is enrolled at a university, holds a job, takes classes, and is not, on paper, among the students most at risk. And still they are not doing as well as they could. They are frustrated. So, often, is their family. Yet at this level, unlike in K-12, teachers no longer talk to parents or guardians. The people who worry most are left out of the conversation.

I know that worry from the other side, too. I have a much younger brother and sister. I know what it is like to watch someone you love grow up and wonder two things: whether they are getting the education they deserve, and whether they are putting in the effort you know they are capable of. Because we love them and believe in them, we sometimes push hard. This letter is what I would like to tell those families: parents, guardians, and anyone who loves a university student who is not doing as well as hoped.

I am not a psychologist or a therapist. I read research for a living. What follows is a set of first steps drawn from that research, each one checked against more than one independent source. It is not a substitute for professional help. If anything, I hope it convinces you to seek it.

Start with something that is easy to forget when you are worried: failure in school is not only an individual story. The Brazilian psychologist Maria Helena Souza Patto spent years [showing](https://doi.org/10.11606/9786587596334) that institutions and social conditions produce much of school failure, and that [blaming the student and the family](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/318558222_Analise_psicossocial_do_fracasso_escolar_na_Educacao_de_Jovens_e_Adultos) hides the role of the school itself. A [review of studies on university dropout](https://www.scielo.br/j/pee/a/KmCm9w8sRMWHxxBFvRQXfXx/?lang=pt) points the same way, with financial problems and unmet expectations among the most common causes.

Two larger forces push in the same direction. First, faith that effort pays has eroded. In 2026, only 49 percent of Americans were [satisfied with the chance to get ahead by working hard](https://www.cnbc.com/make-it/2026/06/29/gen-z-american-dream-eroding-redefining.html), down from 76 percent in 2001. Among the young, the doubt is explicit. In the UK, [76 percent of 18- to 34-year-olds](https://www.theharrispoll.co.uk/media/hard-work-isnt-enough) say hard work pays off less than it did for their parents. A separate worry concerns jobs themselves. Only [43 percent of Americans aged 15 to 34](https://news.gallup.com/poll/708860/young-americans-job-market-pessimism-stands-globally.aspx) think it is a good time to find work, against 64 percent of those 55 and older. That is the widest such gap among the 141 countries Gallup surveyed. Surveys by the résumé site [Zety](https://www.hrdive.com/news/ai-anxiety-gen-z-career-pivot-blue-collar-work/756385/) and by [Gallup](https://www.fastcompany.com/91532708/college-students-choosing-ai-proof-majors-anxiety-rises-over-bleak-u-s-job-market) show that fears about artificial intelligence add to that worry. Doubt about effort and doubt about opportunity are different things, but together they make the old promise sound hollow to many young people.

Second, grades have lost some of their meaning. When universities reward teachers for high student evaluations, grades tend to inflate, and better grades buy better evaluations. The psychologist Wolfgang Stroebe made this case in [2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27899725/) and [2020](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/01973533.2020.1756817), and [a 2021 study](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/02602938.2020.1821866) measured the link.

None of this means your belief in hard work is wrong. Effort still matters. But the young adult you love is growing up in a system that makes its payoff look unreliable. You cannot fix that system. You can change how you respond at home.

What helps at home? In [one study of 403 young adults](https://scholarsarchive.byu.edu/facpub/4350/), those whose parents were warm, explained their reasons, and gave room to choose, with little control, had the most positive outcomes. A [study of 680 college students](https://www.mdpi.com/2673-995X/4/2/35) described responsiveness and autonomy support as developmentally appropriate and adaptive at this age. A [study of 287 Chinese university students](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.735570/full) found the same pattern. Autonomy support went with healthier motivation, while psychological control went with frustrated psychological needs. This result suggests the finding is not limited to Western families.

Research in Portuguese points to something subtle. Brazilian adolescents who had never failed a grade [saw their parents as more emotionally available, attentive, and interested](https://www.redalyc.org/journal/1171/117157485008/html/). Students who feel their family values their studies [tend to do better](https://repositorio.ispa.pt/server/api/core/bitstreams/0c2df61b-7425-44f1-bd5b-cad708e5ae7a/content). But the quality of involvement matters more than the amount. A [review of 63 studies](https://doi.org/10.2307/353560) of fathers who lived apart from their children, cited in [Brazilian research on fathers](http://www.scielo.br/j/paideia/a/SSr8VgZ73jXCBHtHgq4B78y/?lang=pt), found that how often fathers saw their children mattered less than what they did together. Closeness and authoritative parenting went with better school results. How often they saw each other did not. Results also [worsen when parents always step in to help](https://www.scielo.br/j/pee/a/WH4ZWCfNNvsKM4ZwZjQBWNM/?format=html&lang=pt), "reducing their children's autonomy." If you are already very involved, the question is not whether to care more. It is which kinds of care to keep and which to let go.

That question becomes urgent when a young adult is truly stuck. [Researchers at Yale](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4895190/) have studied young adults who stay highly dependent on their families. They note that people often dismiss these young adults as "overly pampered and lazy" and their parents as "too indulgent." The researchers reject that view. Their model is different. Out of love, families often rearrange life so that the young adult can avoid what feels frightening. This "accommodation" is not spoiling. In my own view, even devoted, demanding guardians can fall into it without noticing. But accommodation can quietly keep someone stuck.

Their response is a program called SPACE that works through the guardian, because the young adult is often [unwilling to engage in treatment directly](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04704583). Guardians learn to reduce accommodation step by step while expressing both acceptance and confidence. An [early, small trial](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/21676968241252851) of an approach the same group [described in 2022](https://guilfordjournals.com/doi/10.1521/bumc.2022.86.3.249) found promising results. Clinicians outside Yale give consistent advice. Set clear expectations, validate feelings, and hand back responsibility in manageable steps, [advises the Child Mind Institute](https://childmind.org/article/failure-to-launch-syndrome/). Be specific about expectations and timeframes, [says the nonprofit CHADD](https://chadd.org/attention-article/failure-to-launch-addressing-the-needs-of-transition-age-young-adults/). And tell them plainly what support you are willing to give, because [over-rescuing can undermine growth](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/liking-the-child-you-love/202412/helping-or-hurting-3-tips-for-parents-of-struggling-adults).

A caveat: this research is about young adults who are neither working nor studying. The students I described at the start work and study, so it fits them only in part. I include it because its core idea, that loving help can unintentionally keep someone in place, seems worth knowing even in milder cases. That last point is my reading, not the researchers' claim.

The students I see most often are not stuck at home. They are in school, failing classes or close to losing their place. Here, research on universities offers a lesson. When a student lands on academic probation, the wording of the university's letter matters. At Stanford and elsewhere, [letters that addressed shame and belonging](https://purl.stanford.edu/xq150gy8094) reduced feelings of shame and intentions to drop out. In one study, these students were later [more likely to recover and stay enrolled](https://www.ihep.org/publication/student-experience-and-belonging-strong-outcomes/), although the researcher described that evidence as inconsistent. A [college in Canada](https://www.mohawknewsdesk.ca/a-simple-intervention-that-supports-future-success-of-students-on-academic-probation/) reported similar results with the same research group, and [a trial of a letter redesigned for clarity](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42522508/) found smaller gains.

One detail stands out. The standard letter promised help with "whatever difficulties you may be experiencing." According to the [lead researcher](https://www.mohawkcollege.ca/college-student-success-innovation-centre/publications-and-resources/psychologically-attuned), that kind of wording does not work well with students on probation, however kind it sounds. These studies are about institutions, not families, so the parallel is mine. My suggestion is to skip the general offer of help. Name one concrete problem, such as a missed deadline or a class that is slipping, and ask what would help with that one thing.

Behind failing grades there is often something harder to watch: someone you love who stops trying. Research on self-worth offers a kinder reading. When failure feels like proof of low ability, [withholding effort protects self-worth](https://competencycultivation.uta.edu/files/2021/07/Construction-and-Initial-Validation-of-the-Self-Worth-Protection-Scale-Thompson-Dinnel.pdf). Put in my own words, "I didn't really try" hurts less than "I tried and failed." The psychologist Martin Covington [put it this way](https://principlesoflearning.wordpress.com/dissertation/chapter-3-literature-review-2/the-human-perspective/self-worth-theory-covington-1976/): "the reluctant learner who may refuse to study is already motivated, driven by circumstances to protect his or her self-esteem." [Fear of being judged](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s44202-025-00572-9) is linked to the same pattern. This is protection, not laziness.

This is also why guilt may backfire. Guilt induction means appealing to your own sacrifice, disappointment, or distress to steer a child's behavior. [Researchers class it](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1900170/full) as a form of psychological control. In [one study](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/221973996_Parental_psychological_control_and_dysfunctional_separation-individuation_A_tale_of_two_different_dynamics), psychologically controlling parenting went with problematic separation from parents, in the form of both excessive dependence and excessive independence. A clinician writing for parents, the psychologist Jeffrey Bernstein, reaches a similar conclusion from practice: "[projecting guilt only widens emotional gaps](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/liking-the-child-you-love/202508/when-your-adult-child-is-angry-with-you)." Culture may moderate the effect. In a [vignette study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5858522/), Belgian adolescents rated guilt induction as just as controlling as other forms of control. Chinese adolescents rated it as less controlling and less need-frustrating than other forms of control. In my view, a young adult raised largely in a culture different from the guardian's may not share whatever buffer the guardian's culture provides.

One more idea comes with a grain of salt. Bernstein [suggests](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/liking-the-child-you-love/202511/when-your-adult-child-lashes-out-heres-why-and-what-to-do) that struggling young adults often project their frustration onto their parents. In his view, lashing out may reflect an old inner voice saying they are "never enough." This is one practitioner's view, not tested research. I include it because it may help you take an angry moment less personally, not because it is established.

None of this replaces professional help. Everything here is a first step, and a professional can go further. Parent-focused programs such as SPACE exist because guardians can change the dynamic even when the young adult will not come to therapy. Consider looking for a clinician trained in this kind of work.

So where does this leave you? What to accept: this is a complicated problem, with many things in play at once. Schools, the economy, and the times all play a part, and so do the choices of the young adult and of the people around them. It is not only the young adult's fault, and it is not only yours. Let go of the idea that either of you can fix it alone. Nobody should carry all of this weight by themselves.

What to do now: stay connected and show specific interest. Drop guilt and reminders of sacrifice. Set calm, specific limits about what you will and will not provide. Hand over choices gradually. Instead of a general offer of help, name one concrete problem and ask what would help with it. Expect slow progress, not overnight change. And consult a professional.

Being stuck is not the end of the story. With patience, it is often the middle.
