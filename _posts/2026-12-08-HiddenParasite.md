# One blood sample, two genomes

A genome project sequences everything in the animal's blood, not only the animal. Last week we wrote about the genomes of three island lanceheads from Brazil. While we cleaned the genome of one of them, *Bothrops sazimai*, we found reads from *Hepatozoon*. This single-celled blood parasite is the one most often reported in snakes. Instead of throwing those reads away, we assembled them.

## What we found

The parasite belongs to a *Hepatozoon* lineage that no described species matches. Under the microscope, its blood stages (gamonts) combine a short, broad body with a long, narrow nucleus.

Its mitochondrial genome is a circular molecule of 10,479 base pairs. That is 63% longer than any *Hepatozoon* mitochondrial genome published before, and its genes come in an order never seen in the genus. We also recovered the complete genome of its apicoplast (32,316 base pairs), a small organelle that this group of parasites inherited from an ancient alga. All of this came from data we already had, at no extra sequencing cost.

## Who did the work

Giselle Pessanha Pessoa presented this work at Genética 2026, the 71st Brazilian Congress of Genetics, in Florianópolis. Igor Salles de Oliveira, [Giovanna Yumi Scorsim Omura](https://phyloinformatics.com/members/Giovanna_Yumi_Scorsim_Omura.html), Milton Yutaka Nishiyama Junior, Maria José de Jesus Silva, and I are her coauthors. CAPES funded the work.

## How this fits our lab

Two of our research lines meet in this project. One follows pathogens and parasites. The other reads the genomes of animals that few people study. Other *Hepatozoon* species infect frogs, lizards, and mammals, including dogs. This project shows that genome data deserve a second look: once for the animal, and once for whatever lives inside it.
