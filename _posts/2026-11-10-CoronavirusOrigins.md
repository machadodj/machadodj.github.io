# A family tree of 2,006 coronaviruses points to bats

SARS-CoV reached people in 2002, MERS-CoV in 2012, and SARS-CoV-2 in 2019. Each virus caused a deadly outbreak. Each one also descends from coronaviruses that live in bats. We showed this with a family tree of 2,006 complete coronavirus genomes, which Rachel Scott, Sayal Guirales, Daniel Janies, and I published in *Cladistics*.

## How a family tree finds the source

A phylogenetic tree groups viruses by shared ancestry, the way a family tree groups relatives. We built ours from whole genomes. We used the gene annotations to line up each gene with its counterpart in every other virus, and then we analyzed all the genes together. The two groups of coronaviruses that started in birds served as the outgroup, the reference point that roots the tree. With the root in place, we traced how coronaviruses jumped between hosts across ten orders of animals.

The tree confirms that bat coronaviruses are the closest relatives of SARS-CoV, SARS-CoV-2, and the MERS-related viruses. It also counts the other jumps between animals, which shows where the risk of new human coronaviruses comes from. A second data set of 250 SARS-CoV-2 genomes clarified how the COVID-19 virus relates to its bat relatives.

## Why the tree matters

New coronaviruses will keep jumping from animals to people. A tree we can trust tells us which hosts to watch and which viruses sit one step away from us. When a new coronavirus appears, a tree like this one is the first place we put it.

## How this fits our lab

This paper shows what we mean by phyloinformatics. We join evolutionary trees, genomes, and machine learning to answer practical questions about the health of people, animals, and the environment. Viruses are the hardest test for these tools, so much of our work starts there. The same tools also read the genomes of poison frogs, island snakes, brittle stars, and museum specimens.
