# Young islands, ancient jumping genes

Three lancehead snakes live only on small islands off southeastern Brazil: *Bothrops alcatraz*, *B. insularis*, and *B. sazimai*. Rising seas cut those islands off from the mainland within the last 11,000 to 55,000 years. All three species are endangered.

With our partners at Instituto Butantan and the University of São Paulo, we assembled the first genomes of all three species. We sequenced them with Oxford Nanopore long reads at UNC Charlotte. Then we compared their repeated DNA with that of the jararaca (*B. jararaca*), their relative on the mainland.

## What we found

Jumping genes (transposable elements) are pieces of DNA that copy themselves into new places in the genome. Repeated DNA, including jumping genes, now fills about half of each of these snake genomes.

Island life did not rewrite these genomes. The four species carry repeats in nearly identical proportions. Every major burst of jumping genes happened millions of years before the islands formed. A few families of jumping genes do show signs of renewed activity near the present, and some of them only in the island species. Those families are the next place to look.

## Who did the work

## How this fits our lab

Genomes of animals that few people study, such as island snakes and poison frogs, are full of repeats and hard to assemble. Reading them takes the same evolutionary tools we use on viruses. Next week we tell the second story hidden in these data: a parasite we found in the blood of one of the snakes.
